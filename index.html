How I Work
01 — Discovery
Discovery before assumptions

Before anything gets designed or built, I run a structured discovery phase. That means user and operational interviews, competitive mapping, and tailored workshops designed specifically for each project and each goal. Not generic exercises. Every activity is chosen to extract something concrete: constraints, mental models, workflow gaps, decision owners.

The output is always a detailed requirements list paired with a roadmap the whole team has contributed to. That shared ownership matters. When everyone has been part of building the picture, you eliminate the back-and-forth, the bottlenecks, and the “I thought we agreed on...” conversations. From that baseline, design and engineering can work in parallel without waiting on each other. In practice, this approach reduced scope creep by 45% across projects and compressed delivery cycles by 60%. The UGT enrollment system went from a 6-month industry standard to a 2-month delivery.

User interviews JTBD Journey mapping Design sprints Service blueprints Stakeholder workshops Opportunity mapping
02 — Prioritization
A prioritization framework I actually use

I prioritize with RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) cross-referenced against the current quarter’s OKRs. When two initiatives tie, the tiebreaker is: which one unblocks the most? Every decision gets logged in the backlog with a one-line rationale so six weeks later nobody has to reconstruct why something got bumped.

Because I come from product design, I can pressure-test priorities against what’s actually feasible to ship well, not just feasible to build. A feature that’s technically possible but creates a UX dead end isn’t a win. That lens keeps roadmaps from drifting toward complexity for complexity’s sake.

RICE scoring OKR alignment Backlog hygiene Now / Next / Later
03 — Negotiation
Stakeholder vs. Engineering tension

When a stakeholder pushes for a feature that engineering says is expensive, I separate the conversation about what from when. It usually becomes a scoping problem: can we validate the core assumption with 20% of the effort? I’ve shipped constrained versions of features that proved or disproved the hypothesis fast enough to make the full investment decision easier for everyone involved.

My design background helps here in a specific way: I can sketch the constrained version quickly enough that the conversation stops being abstract. When people can see what the MVP actually looks like, the negotiation gets unstuck.

Scope negotiation MVP scoping Rapid prototyping Data-driven decisions
04 — Specs
Specs that hold scope

My specs include the problem statement, the user segment affected, the success metric being moved, edge cases the team hasn’t considered yet, and explicit non-goals. If it’s not written down, it’s in scope by default. User stories alone aren’t enough; I add acceptance criteria and flag dependencies before a ticket enters the sprint.

Writing tight specs is where the Design Thinking side shows up in the documentation layer. Framing the problem correctly before writing a single requirement saves more time than any tool or process downstream.

User stories Acceptance criteria Non-goals Dependency mapping
05 — Delivery
Backlog and release management

A backlog with 200 unsorted tickets doesn’t work. I keep a living backlog with clear status tiers: Now, Next, Later, Parked. Parked is where good ideas go when there’s no bandwidth, not the trash. Every sprint planning starts with: what changed since last time that should move something?

Release management means communicating status to stakeholders before they ask, not after they notice something is late. And because I can read a design in progress and catch UX problems before they reach QA, release cycles tend to have fewer last-minute surprises.

Sprint planning Release management Stakeholder comms Retrospectives
06 — Intersection
Where product management and design meet

I work at the intersection of both disciplines and I don’t think of one as primary. Product management gives me the framework: discovery, prioritization, roadmap, stakeholder alignment, delivery. Product design gives me the tools to work inside that framework with more precision than most PMs can: I can run a usability session, catch a broken flow in a spec review, build a prototype to unblock a negotiation, or brief a designer with enough specificity that the first iteration lands close.

In practice that looks like: running discovery and coming back with both a prioritized requirements list and a validated interaction model. Sitting in a technical review and flagging UX debt before it ships. Knowing when a roadmap decision will create design complexity downstream, before the sprint starts. Neither role is the fallback. Both are the job.

Problem framing Roadmap ownership Design systems UX audit Prototyping Cross-functional leadership
